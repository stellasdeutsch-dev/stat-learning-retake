Chapter 08 min · BasicsMust

Five moves solve almost the whole exam.

Every task in both 2026 exams was one of five moves, applied to a different distribution. Learn the words and the moves here. Every later chapter is practice of these moves.

The six words you need

Parameter \(\theta\)

The unknown number. The chance a coin shows heads, or the average goals per match \(\lambda\). Also called \(p, q, \lambda, \eta\).

Data \(D\)

What you observed: \(D=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}\). Independent data means you multiply the probabilities.

Likelihood \(p(D\mid\theta)\)

How well a value of \(\theta\) explains the data. Same formula as the model, read as a function of \(\theta\).

Prior \(p(\theta)\)

What you believe about \(\theta\) before the data. Usually Beta or Gamma.

Posterior \(p(\theta\mid D)\)

Your belief after the data. Always: posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior.

\(\propto\) “proportional to”

Equal up to a constant. You may throw away every factor that does not contain \(\theta\): \(k!\), binomial coefficients, \(\Gamma(a)\), \(b^a\).

The five moves

  1. MLE (maximum likelihood estimate). Take the log of the likelihood, differentiate, set it to 0, solve for \(\theta\).
    Hook: log, derivative, zero.
  2. Posterior. Multiply likelihood by prior, drop constants, add the exponents, recognise the family (Beta or Gamma).
    Hook: multiply, drop, add, recognise.
  3. Point estimates. The posterior mean (the average) or the MAP (the peak, which is the mode). Read them from the family’s formula.
  4. Predictive. The probability of the next observation: average the model over the posterior, \(p(x_{\text{new}}\mid D)=\int p(x_{\text{new}}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta\mid D)\,d\theta\).
  5. Classify. For each class compute score \(=\log(\text{prior}) + \log p(x\mid\text{class})\). The largest score wins.

See all five on one coin

You flip a coin \(n=10\) times and see \(S=7\) heads. One flip has probability \(\theta^{x}(1-\theta)^{1-x}\) with \(x\in\{0,1\}\).

1 · MLE. Likelihood \(\theta^{7}(1-\theta)^{3}\). Log: \(\ell(\theta)=7\log\theta+3\log(1-\theta)\).

\[\ell'(\theta)=\frac{7}{\theta}-\frac{3}{1-\theta}=0\;\Rightarrow\;7(1-\theta)=3\theta\;\Rightarrow\;\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}=\frac{7}{10}.\]

2 · Posterior. Prior \(\mathrm{Beta}(2,2)\propto\theta^{1}(1-\theta)^{1}\). Multiply and add exponents:

\[p(\theta\mid D)\propto\theta^{7}(1-\theta)^{3}\cdot\theta^{1}(1-\theta)^{1}=\theta^{8}(1-\theta)^{4}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathrm{Beta}(9,5).\]

3 · Estimates. Mean \(\frac{9}{9+5}=0.64\). MAP \(\frac{9-1}{9+5-2}=\frac{8}{12}=0.67\).

4 · Predictive. \(P(\text{next flip is heads}\mid D)=\) posterior mean \(=0.64\).

5 · Classify is the same idea with classes instead of \(\theta\). You will see it in chapters 7 and 8.

Chapter 112 min · MidtermMust

Distributions to know by heart.

The midterm opens with “define this distribution”. The whole exam uses the same dozen formulas. Learn them here, then switch on quiz mode and test yourself.

Asked

Midterm 2026, 1(a): Define the Beta distribution by giving the density. Don’t forget to specify the range of values.
Midterm 2025, 1(a–b): Define the Binomial distribution. Give its conjugate prior by its density.

Write this

For parameters \(a\gt 0,\ b\gt 0\):

\[p(\theta\mid a,b)=\frac{1}{B(a,b)}\,\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}\quad\text{for }\theta\in[0,1],\qquad p(\theta)=0\text{ otherwise},\]

with \(B(a,b)=\frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}\). Mean \(\frac{a}{a+b}\).

Remember

Always write three things: the formula, the range of the variable, the range of the parameters. Add the mean for a bonus.

The dozen

Bernoulli(\(\theta\)) one coin flip

\[\theta^{x}(1-\theta)^{1-x},\quad x\in\{0,1\}\]

\(\theta\in[0,1]\). Mean \(\theta\), variance \(\theta(1-\theta)\).

Binomial(\(N,\theta\)) successes in N flips

\[\binom{N}{k}\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{N-k},\quad k=0,\dots,N\]

\(\theta\in[0,1]\). Mean \(N\theta\). \(\binom Nk=\frac{N!}{k!(N-k)!}\).

Geometric, trials tries until first success

\[p(1-p)^{k-1},\quad k=1,2,\dots\]

\(p\in(0,1]\). Mean \(\frac1p\). Memoryless.

Geometric, failures failures before success

\[q(1-q)^{k},\quad k=0,1,2,\dots\]

Mean \(\frac{1-q}{q}\). Same model, counted from 0.

Negative binomial failures before r-th success

\[\binom{k+r-1}{k}p^{r}(1-p)^{k},\quad k=0,1,\dots\]

\(r\) known. Mean \(\frac{r(1-p)}{p}\). \(r=1\) gives the geometric.

Poisson(\(\lambda\)) counts: goals, calls

\[e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!},\quad k=0,1,2,\dots\]

\(\lambda\gt 0\). Mean \(=\) variance \(=\lambda\).

Exponential(\(\lambda\)) waiting times

\[\lambda e^{-\lambda x},\quad x\ge 0\]

\(\lambda\gt 0\). Mean \(\frac1\lambda\). \(P(X\gt t)=e^{-\lambda t}\).

Gaussian \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) measurements

\[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}},\quad x\in\mathbb R\]

Mean \(\mu\), variance \(\sigma^2\gt 0\).

Beta(\(a,b\)) a probability

\[\frac{\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}}{B(a,b)},\quad\theta\in[0,1]\]

\(a,b\gt 0\). Mean \(\frac{a}{a+b}\), mode \(\frac{a-1}{a+b-2}\).

Gamma(\(a,b\)) a positive rate

\[\frac{b^{a}}{\Gamma(a)}\lambda^{a-1}e^{-b\lambda},\quad\lambda\gt 0\]

\(a,b\gt 0\). Mean \(\frac ab\), mode \(\frac{a-1}{b}\), variance \(\frac{a}{b^2}\).

Dirichlet(\(\alpha\)) a probability vector

\[\frac{\Gamma(\sum_k\alpha_k)}{\prod_k\Gamma(\alpha_k)}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\theta_k^{\alpha_k-1}\]

\(\theta_k\ge0,\ \sum_k\theta_k=1\). Mean \(\frac{\alpha_k}{\sum_j\alpha_j}\).

Multivariate Gaussian vectors in \(\mathbb R^D\)

\[\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}e^{-\frac12(x-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)}\]

\(\Sigma\) symmetric, positive definite. \(|\Sigma|=\det\Sigma\).

Warm-up questions: a conditional probability with dice, the memoryless property, Bayes’ theorem, and a few definitions. Each has one recipe.

Conditional probability by counting

Midterm 2026, 1(b)

A fair die has sides {A, B, C}, each with probability 1/3. It is rolled twice. Calculate the probability that A occurs twice, given that it occurred at least once.

Draw all \(3\times3=9\) equally likely outcomes. Blue cells contain at least one A. The green cell is AA.

ABC AAAABAC BBABBBC CCACBCC
  1. Definition: \(P(E\mid F)=\dfrac{P(E\cap F)}{P(F)}\). “A twice” already implies “A at least once”, so \(P(E\cap F)=P(AA)\).
  2. \(P(AA)=\frac13\cdot\frac13=\frac19\).
  3. \(P(\text{at least one A})=1-P(\text{no A})=1-\left(\frac23\right)^2=\frac59\).
  4. \(P(AA\mid\text{at least one A})=\dfrac{1/9}{5/9}=\dfrac15\).
Remember

Shrink the world to the condition, then count: 1 good cell out of 5.

Trap

\(\frac13\) is the answer to a different question: “A twice, given the first roll is A”. “At least once” allows the A in either roll.

If they change it

A die with \(m\) sides, two rolls: \(\dfrac{1/m^2}{1-(1-1/m)^2}=\dfrac{1}{2m-1}\). Four sides: \(\frac17\). Six sides: \(\frac1{11}\). More rolls: list or count outcomes the same way.

Bayes’ theorem, marginal and conditional

Final 2026 1(a) · Final 2025 1(a) · Midterm 2025 1(c)

State Bayes’ theorem for densities. Give the posterior \(p(\lambda\mid D)\). Give the density of \(X\), and of \(X\) given \(Y=y\).

Write this \[p(\theta\mid D)=\frac{p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(D)},\qquad p(D)=\int p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta.\] \[\text{marginal: } p(x)=\int p(x,y)\,dy,\qquad\text{conditional: } p(x\mid y)=\frac{p(x,y)}{p(y)}\ \ (p(y)\gt 0).\]
Remember

Posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence. Marginal = integrate the other variable out. Conditional = joint / marginal. For discrete variables replace \(\int\) by \(\sum\).

The memoryless property

Midterm 2026, 1(c)

\(P(X=k)=p(1-p)^{k-1}\), \(k\in\{1,2,\dots\}\). Calculate \(P(X\ge n+m\mid X\ge n)\). Hint: \(\sum_{k\ge L}p(1-p)^{k-1}=(1-p)^{L-1}\sum_{k\ge1}p(1-p)^{k-1}\). The last sum is 1, but why?

  1. Why the sum is 1. It adds the probabilities of all possible values of \(X\). All probabilities together give 1. (Or: geometric series \(p\cdot\frac{1}{1-(1-p)}=1\).)
  2. Tail. By the hint, \(P(X\ge L)=(1-p)^{L-1}\). In words: the first \(L-1\) tries all failed.
  3. Intersection. \(\{X\ge n+m\}\) is inside \(\{X\ge n\}\), so the intersection is just \(\{X\ge n+m\}\).
  4. Divide. \[P(X\ge n+m\mid X\ge n)=\frac{(1-p)^{n+m-1}}{(1-p)^{n-1}}=(1-p)^{m}.\]
Conclusion to write

The answer \((1-p)^m\) does not depend on \(n\). Having already waited \(n\) tries changes nothing. The geometric distribution is memoryless.

If they change it

Exponential: \(P(X\gt t)=\int_t^\infty\lambda e^{-\lambda x}dx=e^{-\lambda t}\), so \(P(X\gt s+t\mid X\gt s)=\frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}}=e^{-\lambda t}\). Geometric counted from 0 (\(q(1-q)^k\)): \(P(X\ge L)=(1-q)^{L}\), same conclusion.

Remember

The coin has no memory.

Independent vs uncorrelated

Final 2026, 5 (true/false)

There exists a pair \((X,Y)\) with correlation 0 but \(X\) and \(Y\) dependent. True.

Take \(X\) uniform on \([-1,1]\) and \(Y=X^2\). Then \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=E[X^3]-E[X]E[X^2]=0-0=0\), but \(Y\) is completely determined by \(X\). Correlation only measures straight-line dependence; a parabola has none.

  • Independent \(\Rightarrow\) uncorrelated (always).
  • Uncorrelated \(\Rightarrow\) independent only for jointly Gaussian variables.
Remember

Parabola: dependent, but zero correlation.

Exponential family

Midterm 2025, 1(d)

Give one distribution in the exponential family (not Gamma or Exponential) and one that is not.

Form: \(p(x\mid\theta)=h(x)\exp\big(\eta(\theta)\,T(x)-A(\theta)\big)\), and the support (where \(p\gt 0\)) must not depend on \(\theta\).

Write this

In: Poisson, because \(e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^x}{x!}=\frac{1}{x!}\exp(x\log\lambda-\lambda)\). Also Bernoulli, Binomial (fixed \(N\)), Gaussian, Beta.

Out: Uniform on \([0,\theta]\): density \(\frac1\theta\) on \([0,\theta]\). Its support moves with \(\theta\). Also Student-t, Cauchy, Gaussian mixtures.

Chapter 325 min · Midterm and FinalMust

The posterior task. It comes every time.

Six exams in a row had the same task with a different distribution: Negative binomial, Poisson, Geometric, Exponential, even an unknown lifetime density. The steps never change. This is the most valuable chapter.

Asked
  • Midterm 2026, 2: Negative binomial + Beta: MLE, posterior, mean, predictive.
  • Midterm 2026, 3: Poisson (goals) + Gamma: likelihood constant, posterior, prior mode, MAP.
  • Final 2026, 2: Geometric “one more drink” \(\lambda(1-\lambda)^{y-1}\): choose a prior, compute the posterior.
  • Final 2025, 3 and Midterm 2025, 3 and Retake 2025, 3: Geometric, Exponential, Gompertz. Same steps.

The template

  1. Likelihood. Multiply over all data points. Collect the parameter into powers. Everything without the parameter is the constant.
  2. MLE. \(\ell=\log L\). Solve \(\ell'=0\).
  3. Posterior. Likelihood × prior. Add exponents. Recognise Beta or Gamma and read off the new parameters.
  4. Mean / MAP / mode. Plug the new parameters into the family’s formulas.
  5. Predictive. \(\int p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta\mid D)d\theta\) = model constant × ratio of normalising constants (chapter 4).

Remember

“a gets the successes (or events), b gets the failures (or exposure).” Beta counts successes vs failures. Gamma counts events vs time or matches.

Worked: Poisson + Gamma (Midterm 2026, Task 3)

Goals per match: \(p(k\mid\lambda)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\). Prior \(p(\lambda)=\frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)}\lambda^{a-1}e^{-b\lambda}\).

(a) Show \(p(D\mid\lambda)\propto e^{-n\lambda}\lambda^S\). Find the constant.

\[p(D\mid\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^n e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{k_i}}{k_i!}=\frac{1}{k_1!\cdots k_n!}\;e^{-n\lambda}\,\lambda^{S}.\]

\(n\) factors \(e^{-\lambda}\) give \(e^{-n\lambda}\); the powers add to \(S\). The constant is \(\dfrac{1}{\prod_i k_i!}\).

(b) Posterior.

\[p(\lambda\mid D)\propto e^{-n\lambda}\lambda^{S}\cdot\lambda^{a-1}e^{-b\lambda}=\lambda^{a+S-1}e^{-(b+n)\lambda}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathrm{Gamma}(a+S,\ b+n).\]

(c) Mode of the prior (\(a\gt1\)). Log, derivative, zero:

\[\frac{d}{d\lambda}\big[(a-1)\log\lambda-b\lambda\big]=\frac{a-1}{\lambda}-b=0\;\Rightarrow\;\lambda=\frac{a-1}{b}.\]

Second derivative \(-\frac{a-1}{\lambda^2}\lt 0\), so it is a maximum.

(d) MAP = mode of the posterior. Use (c) with the new parameters: \(\ \hat\lambda_{\text{MAP}}=\dfrac{a+S-1}{b+n}.\)

Numbers: prior Gamma(2, 1), goals {3, 1, 4, 2}: \(n=4,\ S=10\). Posterior Gamma(12, 5). MLE \(2.5\), MAP \(\frac{11}{5}=2.2\), mean \(\frac{12}5=2.4\).

Trap

In the Poisson the rate \(b\) grows by \(n\) (matches), the shape \(a\) grows by \(S\) (goals). Mode \(\frac{a-1}{b}\) is not the mean \(\frac ab\).

Worked: Negative binomial + Beta (Midterm 2026, Task 2)

\(f(k\mid p)=\binom{k+r-1}{k}p^{r}(1-p)^{k}\), \(r\) known. Hint: “things which don’t depend on \(p\) can be … ignored”.

(a) MLE. The binomial coefficient has no \(p\): it is a constant.

\[L(p)\propto p^{nr}(1-p)^{S},\quad\ell(p)=nr\log p+S\log(1-p),\quad\ell'(p)=\frac{nr}{p}-\frac{S}{1-p}=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat p=\frac{nr}{nr+S}.\]

(b) Posterior with prior Beta(a, b):

\[f(p\mid D)\propto p^{nr}(1-p)^{S}\,p^{a-1}(1-p)^{b-1}=p^{a+nr-1}(1-p)^{b+S-1}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathrm{Beta}(a+nr,\ b+S).\]

(c) Posterior mean: \(\ E[p\mid D]=\dfrac{a+nr}{a+b+nr+S}.\)

(d) Predictive. Put \(\alpha=a+nr,\ \beta=b+S\). Here the coefficient stays, because it contains the new \(k\):

\[f(k\mid D)=\int_0^1\binom{k+r-1}{k}p^{r}(1-p)^{k}\,\frac{p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\,dp=\binom{k+r-1}{k}\frac{B(\alpha+r,\ \beta+k)}{B(\alpha,\beta)}.\]
Remember

Successes over (successes + failures): \(nr\) successes in total, \(S\) failures in total. Predictive: “new B over old B”.

Trap

The exponent of \(p\) is \(nr\), not \(n\) or \(r\). Drop the coefficient for the MLE and posterior, but keep it in the predictive.

Worked: Geometric “one more drink” (Final 2026, Task 2)

\(p(y_i\mid\lambda)=\lambda(1-\lambda)^{y_i-1}\), \(y_i\in\{1,2,\dots\}\). Choose a prior, compute the posterior.

Likelihood. \(p(D\mid\lambda)=\prod_i\lambda(1-\lambda)^{y_i-1}=\lambda^{n}(1-\lambda)^{S-n}\), with \(S=\sum_i y_i\).

Prior. \(\lambda\in(0,1)\) is a probability, so choose \(\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)\propto\lambda^{a-1}(1-\lambda)^{b-1}\).

\[p(\lambda\mid D)\propto\lambda^{n+a-1}(1-\lambda)^{S-n+b-1}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathrm{Beta}(a+n,\ b+S-n).\]

Extras if asked: MLE \(\frac nS\), mean \(\frac{a+n}{a+b+S}\), MAP \(\frac{a+n-1}{a+b+S-2}\).

Remember

The exponent of \((1-\lambda)\) counts failures: \(S-n\), because each person keeps the promise exactly once.

Unknown density? Same moves.

Retake 2025 gave a density nobody had seen: the Gompertz lifetime \(f(x\mid\eta)=\eta\,e^{\eta+x-\eta e^{x}}\), \(x\gt0\), with a Gamma(\(a,\lambda\)) prior. Don’t panic. Collect \(\eta\).

(a) With \(S=\sum x_i\) and \(T=\sum e^{x_i}\):

\[f(D\mid\eta)=\prod_i\eta\,e^{\eta+x_i-\eta e^{x_i}}=\eta^{n}e^{n\eta+S-\eta T}=e^{S}\cdot\eta^{n}e^{-\eta(T-n)}.\]

(b) Times the prior \(\eta^{a-1}e^{-\lambda\eta}\):

\[f(\eta\mid D)\propto\eta^{a+n-1}e^{-\eta(\lambda+T-n)}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathrm{Gamma}(a+n,\ \lambda+T-n).\]

(c) With \(n=4,\ S=2,\ T=20,\ a=\lambda=1\): posterior Gamma(5, 17), so \(E[\eta\mid D]=\frac{5}{17}\approx0.29\). This is what the hint about the Gamma mean points to.

Look at the kernel. \(\theta^{(\dots)}(1-\theta)^{(\dots)}\) means a Beta prior. \(\theta^{(\dots)}e^{-(\dots)\theta}\) means a Gamma prior. Then read the new parameters straight off the exponents.

Predictions, bets and the two dice.

After the posterior, the exam asks what happens next: the mean, the MAP, the probability of the next observation, or whether to take a bet.

Three estimates, three meanings

Predictive: average, never plug in

The predictive averages the model over every possible \(\theta\), weighted by the posterior. The integral always becomes a ratio of normalising constants.

Final 2025, 3(b)

COVID infections are geometric \((1-q)^kq\). The posterior is Beta(10, 50). Probability that the next person infects no one?

Write this

\(p(0\mid D)=\int_0^1 q\cdot\mathrm{Beta}(q\mid10,50)\,dq=E[q\mid D]=\frac{10}{10+50}=\frac16.\)

How to get the Poisson \(p(0\mid D)\) in one lineShow
\[\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda}\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta\lambda}d\lambda=\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{(\beta+1)^\alpha}=\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\right)^\alpha.\]

The integral is the Gamma integral from the toolbox with \(b\) replaced by \(\beta+1\).

Should you take the bet?

A bet question is expected value: probability of winning × win, minus probability of losing × loss.

The two dice (Midterm 2025, Task 2)

Die \(h_1\) has faces 1–4, die \(h_2\) has faces 1–8. You see throws, not the die. Only two hypotheses, so every sum has two terms.

  1. Likelihood. \(p(D\mid h_1)=(1/4)^n\) if all throws are ≤ 4, else 0. \(p(D\mid h_2)=(1/8)^n\).
  2. Posterior (uniform prior, all ≤ 4): \(p(h_1\mid D)=\frac{(1/4)^n}{(1/4)^n+(1/8)^n}=\frac{2^n}{2^n+1}\). Each fitting throw doubles the odds for the small die: the size principle.
  3. Model averaging. \(p(x_{n+1}\mid D)=\sum_i p(x_{n+1}\mid h_i)\,p(h_i\mid D)\). For faces 1–4: \(\frac14 w_1+\frac18 w_2\); faces 5–8: \(\frac18 w_2\).
  4. MAP with a strong prior. \(p(h_1)=10^{-6}\), data {3, 4}: \(h_1\): \(10^{-6}\cdot\frac1{16}\approx6\cdot10^{-8}\); \(h_2\): \(\approx\frac1{64}\approx0.016\). MAP picks \(h_2\), even though \(h_1\) is the MLE.
  5. Expected next throw after 15 throws all ≤ 4: \(w_1=\frac{2^{15}}{2^{15}+1}\approx1\), so \(E[X\mid D]=2.5\,w_1+4.5\,w_2\approx2.5\).
Remember

A factor of 4 from the data cannot beat a factor of a million from the prior. MAP = prior × likelihood, not likelihood alone.

The idea

Each class is a Gaussian cloud with its own centre \(\mu_c\) and shape \(\Sigma_c\). A new point goes to the class whose cloud, weighted by the prior \(\pi_c\), is highest at that point:

\[P(y=c\mid x)=\frac{\pi_c\,\mathcal N(x\mid\mu_c,\Sigma_c)}{\sum_{c'}\pi_{c'}\,\mathcal N(x\mid\mu_{c'},\Sigma_{c'})}.\]

Take the log and drop the \((2\pi)^{-D/2}\) that every class shares. That gives the score:

The score \[g_c(x)=\log\pi_c-\tfrac12\log|\Sigma_c|-\tfrac12\,d_c^2,\qquad d_c^2=(x-\mu_c)^T\Sigma_c^{-1}(x-\mu_c).\]

Largest \(g_c\) wins. \(d_c^2\) is the Mahalanobis distance: distance measured in the class’s own shape.

(a) The definitions to write

QDA

Quadratic Discriminant Analysis. Goal: put \(x\) into the class with the highest posterior \(P(y=c\mid x)\propto\pi_c\,\mathcal N(x\mid\mu_c,\Sigma_c)\). Assumption: within each class the data are Gaussian with their own mean \(\mu_c\) and their own covariance \(\Sigma_c\). The boundaries \(g_c=g_{c'}\) are quadratic in \(x\), hence the name.

LDA

Linear Discriminant Analysis. Same as QDA, but all classes share one covariance \(\Sigma\). That is the condition for using it. Then \(x^T\Sigma^{-1}x\) and \(\log|\Sigma|\) are the same in every class and cancel, which leaves a score that is linear in \(x\):

\[\delta_c(x)=x^T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_c-\tfrac12\mu_c^T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_c+\log\pi_c.\]

“Discriminant” = the function that separates (discriminates) the classes. “Linear” = its boundaries are straight lines.

Remember

Same Σ → Linear. Own Σ → Quadratic. Diagonal Σ → Naive.

(b) Classify a point: the recipe

  1. For each class compute \(v=x-\mu_c\).
  2. Compute \(|\Sigma_c|=ad-b^2\) and \(\Sigma_c^{-1}=\frac{1}{ad-b^2}\begin{pmatrix}d & -b\\ -b & a\end{pmatrix}\).

  4. Score \(g_c=\log\pi_c-\frac12\log|\Sigma_c|-\frac12d_c^2\). Largest wins.
  5. If asked for probabilities: \(P(c\mid x)=e^{g_c}/\sum_{c'}e^{g_{c'}}\).
Shortcut

Equal priors and equal determinants: just pick the smallest \(d^2\). Equal \(d^2\) and equal determinants: the prior decides.

(b) Why priors matter for small data

Write this

With small data, some feature never appears in a class, so \(\hat\theta_{jc}=0\) (or 1). One zero kills the whole product: the class gets probability 0 no matter what the other features say, and if every class gets a zero we even get \(\frac00\). This is the zero-count or black swan problem, a form of overfitting.

Priors add pseudo-counts and remove the zeros. Conjugate priors: \(\theta_{jc}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)\) and \(\pi\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_C)\). Posterior means:

\[\bar\theta_{jc}=\frac{N_{jc}+a}{N_c+a+b},\qquad\bar\pi_c=\frac{N_c+\alpha_c}{N+\sum_{c'}\alpha_{c'}}\qquad(\text{Laplace: }a=b=\alpha_c=1).\]
Remember

One zero kills the product. Beta for \(\theta\), Dirichlet for \(\pi\).

Advantages of the Bayes approach (Final 2025, 5c): no zero probabilities, sensible estimates on small data, uncertainty is kept, prior knowledge can enter, and it still trains fast by counting.

Other NBC facts they ask

  • Can one feature influence another? No, not within a class: features are conditionally independent given the class. (They can be dependent overall, through the class.)
  • Danger of MLE with fewer points than \(K\cdot D\)? Many counts are 0, so many \(\hat\theta=0\) or 1: zero probabilities and overfitting.
  • Class prior Dir(1, …, 1) after \(N\) observations? Posterior \(\mathrm{Dir}(1+N_1,\dots,1+N_K)\).
  • Many features? Add logs instead of multiplying, to avoid numbers too small for the computer (underflow).
  • Continuous features? Use a 1D Gaussian per feature and class: Gaussian Naive Bayes (like the cats and dogs in chapter 7).

Mutual information for feature selection

\[I(X_j;Y)=\sum_{x_j,\,c}p(x_j,c)\log\frac{p(x_j,c)}{p(x_j)\,p(c)}.\]

Compute it for each feature with the class label. A feature with the same \(\theta\) in every class tells you nothing about the class and has \(I=0\). Keep the top features, drop the ones with small \(I\).

A straight line squeezed through an S-curve so the output is always a probability. The exams ask three things: its log-likelihood, why it has no formula, and how to fit it.

The model

\[P(y=1\mid x)=\mu=\mathrm{sigm}(w^Tx)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-w^Tx}}\in(0,1).\]
sigmoid: stays inside (0, 1)OLS line on 0/1 data: leaves [0, 1]

Log-likelihood, gradient, Hessian

\[LL(w)=\sum_i\Big[y_i\log\mu_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-\mu_i)\Big],\qquad\mathrm{NLL}=-LL.\] \[g=\nabla\mathrm{NLL}=\sum_i(\mu_i-y_i)\,x_i=X^T(\mu-y),\qquad H=X^TSX,\ \ S=\mathrm{diag}\big(\mu_i(1-\mu_i)\big).\]
Remember

Gradient = (prediction − truth) × input.

Why there is no formula

Set the gradient to zero: \(X^T(\mathrm{sigm}(Xw)-y)=0\). The unknown \(w\) is trapped inside the sigmoid, so the equation is non-linear. Compare linear regression, where \(X^TXw=X^Ty\) is linear and solvable by hand. So we iterate:

AlgorithmStepNeeds
Gradient (steepest) descent\(w_{k+1}=w_k-\eta_k g_k\)gradient only; step size \(\eta\)

The NLL is convex, so any minimum the algorithm finds is the global one.

Linear regression and its relatives.

One model, many variations. The key sentence: Gaussian gives squares, Laplace gives absolute values. On the noise it is the loss; on the weights it is the penalty.

The model and its MLE

\[y_i=w^Tx_i+\varepsilon_i,\quad\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\hat w=(X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty.\]

Maximising the Gaussian likelihood is the same as minimising the residual sum of squares \(\mathrm{RSS}(w)=\sum_i(y_i-w^Tx_i)^2\). So standard linear regression minimises squares, not absolute values (Retake 4a: false).

MLE of the noise variance (Final 2025, 4e)

With \(n\) data points (the \(n\) comes from summing \(n\) copies of \(-\frac12\log\sigma^2\)):

\[\ell(\sigma^2)=-\frac n2\log\sigma^2-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat w^Tx_i)^2.\] \[\frac{d\ell}{d\sigma^2}=-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_i(y_i-\hat w^Tx_i)^2=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat w^Tx_i)^2=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n}.\]

Differentiate with respect to \(\sigma^2\) as one variable. Tip: call it \(v\) while you differentiate.

Ridge (penalised least squares) adds a penalty on the size of the weights: minimise \(\sum_i(y_i-w^Tx_i)^2+\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2\). Solution \(\hat w=(X^TX+\lambda I)^{-1}X^Ty\), always invertible for \(\lambda\gt0\). It shrinks the weights towards 0, which reduces overfitting. Bayesian linear regression

Prior \(w\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2I)\), Gaussian noise. The posterior is Gaussian:

\[\Sigma_N=\Big(\tfrac1{\sigma^2}X^TX+\tfrac1{\tau^2}I\Big)^{-1},\qquad\mu_N=\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\Sigma_NX^Ty\ \ (=\text{ridge solution}).\]
Zellner’s g-prior: the derivation in four linesShow

Prior \(\beta\sim\mathcal N(\mu_\beta,\ g\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1})\), likelihood \(y\mid\beta\sim\mathcal N(X\beta,\sigma^2I)\). Collect the terms in \(\beta\) in the exponent as \(-\frac12[\beta^TA\beta-2\beta^Tb]\); then the posterior is \(\mathcal N(A^{-1}b,\ A^{-1})\).

\[A=\frac{X^TX}{\sigma^2}+\frac{X^TX}{g\sigma^2}=\frac{1+g}{g}\cdot\frac{X^TX}{\sigma^2},\qquad b=\frac{X^Ty}{\sigma^2}+\frac{X^TX\mu_\beta}{g\sigma^2}.\] \[A^{-1}=\frac{g}{1+g}\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1},\qquad A^{-1}b=\frac{g}{1+g}(X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty+\frac{1}{1+g}\mu_\beta.\]

So the mean is \(\frac{g}{1+g}\hat\beta_{\text{OLS}}+\frac1{1+g}\mu_\beta\). \(g\to\infty\): OLS. \(g\to0\): prior mean. \(g=1\): halfway.

Any law “number × known function”

Duffing \(F=-kz+\alpha z^3\): columns \(z_i,\ z_i^3\). Drag \(F=c_1v+c_2v^2\): columns \(v_i,\ v_i^2\). \(y=a\sin t+bt^2\): columns \(\sin t_i,\ t_i^2\). Add a column of ones for an intercept. Then \(\hat\beta=(X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty\). Not linear if the parameter sits inside the function, like \(e^{kz}\).

Optimisation facts (Retake 2025 true/false)

Both 2025 finals ended with this task. The bag of words forgets the order. The Markov chain remembers the last word.

Bag of words (Final 2025, 6a)

Write this

Model: each word is \(x_i\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\theta)\) over \(K\) words, independent. Only counts \(N_k\) matter: \(p(D\mid\theta)=\prod_k\theta_k^{N_k}\).

Prior: \(\theta\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_K)\). Posterior: \(\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_1+N_1,\dots,\alpha_K+N_K)\).

Next word: \(P(x_{n+1}=k\mid D)=\frac{\alpha_k+N_k}{\sum_j\alpha_j+n}\).

Good for document classification (spam), bad for predicting the next word, because it ignores order.

Markov chain

The next word depends only on the current word. Parameters: start distribution \(\pi_i=P(X_0=i)\) and transition matrix \(A_{ij}=P(X_{t+1}=j\mid X_t=i)\). Row = from, column = to. Each row sums to 1.

  • MLE = count and divide: \(\hat\pi_i=\frac{\#\text{sentences starting with }i}{\#\text{sentences}}\), \(\hat A_{ij}=\frac{N_{ij}}{\sum_{j'}N_{ij'}}\).
  • One step: read \(A_{ij}\). Two steps: \((A^2)_{ij}=\sum_kA_{ik}A_{kj}\).
  • A whole sequence: \(\pi(x_0)\prod_tA_{x_t,x_{t+1}}\). So \(\pi\) and \(A\) give the full distribution (Retake 4f: true).

Chapter 125 min · FinalGood to know

Gaussian extras.

Smaller lecture topics that can appear as a true/false or a one-line question.

Linear transformations

If \(X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)\), then \(Y=AX+b\sim\mathcal N(A\mu+b,\ A\Sigma A^T)\).

Example: \(X,Y\) independent \(\mathcal N(0,1)\). \((X+Y,X-Y)\) has covariance \(\begin{pmatrix}2 & 0\\ 0 & 2\end{pmatrix}\). \((X+Y,X+2Y)\) has covariance \(\begin{pmatrix}2 & 3\\ 3 & 5\end{pmatrix}\).

Marginals and conditionals

Split \(x=(x_1,x_2)\). The marginal of \(x_1\) is \(\mathcal N(\mu_1,\Sigma_{11})\): just read the block. The conditional is Gaussian too:

\[x_1\mid x_2\sim\mathcal N\big(\mu_1+\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2-\mu_2),\ \ \Sigma_{11}-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}\big).\]

Learning a Gaussian mean (known \(\sigma^2\))

Prior \(\mu\sim\mathcal N(\mu_0,\tau_0^2)\), \(n\) data with average \(\bar x\). Precisions (1 / variance) add, and the mean is a precision-weighted average:

\[\frac1{\tau_n^2}=\frac1{\tau_0^2}+\frac{n}{\sigma^2},\qquad\mu_n=\tau_n^2\Big(\frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2}+\frac{n\bar x}{\sigma^2}\Big).\]

This is the same idea as sensor fusion: a more reliable measurement gets more weight.

Gaussian interpolation (Retake 2025, 4d)

The lecture puts a smoothness prior on the unknown function values that penalises second differences \(x_j-2x_{j+1}+x_{j+2}\) (curvature). With noise-free data the posterior mean passes exactly through the data points and, among all such curves, has the smallest total squared curvature. It is a smooth curve, not a set of straight lines. The uncertainty is 0 at the data points and grows between them.

Answer to the true/false

“The result is straight lines between data points”: false. The curvature prior gives a smooth curve through the points. With noisy data the curve no longer passes exactly through them.
